Our Project Statement

For our machine learning project, we wanted to train a model that could classify images. We also wanted to think about how machine learning systems are created and the ethical challenges that accompany them. Joy Buolamwini's "Unmasking AI" had a significant impact on how we approached this assignment. Throughout her book, Buolamwini demonstrates that artificial intelligence systems are not inherently objective. Rather, they are shaped by the deliberate choices, assumptions, and biases of the people who create them. As we planned, collected data for, and trained our model, we found ourselves returning to lessons from Unmasking AI and considering how they can be used in our own work.

Our project focused on training a machine learning model to differentiate between three common hand gestures with palms facing the camera: an open hand, a two-finger peace sign, and a closed fist with the thumb pointing upward. At first glance, this seemed like a straightforward classification task. However, one of the major takeaways from Buolamwini's book is that even seemingly simple datasets can contain bias. As Buolamwini emphasizes throughout the book, the consequences of bias often begin with small design choices that developers overlook during the creation process. This encouraged us to think carefully about the data we collected, how we collected it, and the limitations of our project.

As we created our model, we encountered another lesson from Unmasking AI, the challenge of creating a representative dataset. Buolamwini repeatedly describes how many AI systems fail because the data used to train them does not adequately represent the diversity of the people who will ultimately use them. While discussing our data collection process, we realized that our group consisted of three light-skin men. This immediately raised concerns about the diversity of the data we would be able to provide, as well as how well our model would work on someone with a darker skin tone.

Having an iteration process where our model can be tested by a diverse group of peers will be vital to creating an unbiased machine. Getting a variety of testers to try out the algorithm and give us feedback on how it performs allows us to get perspectives we would not have access to on our own. This is what Ellen Pao discussed at the conclusion of her essay, where she encouraged tech to take a "do no harm" approach. If we were a company launching this commercially, financial incentives would tell us to launch the product as soon as possible. However, getting a diverse group of testers to iterate on the project before it is used by the masses not only creates a better, more accurate product, but a more equitable one as well.

Another concern that was raised in the data collection process was the background of images. The model had to be able to distinguish between the intended hand sign and a high-contrast or distracting background. As we collected data, we tried to get a variety of light levels and backgrounds in our images to ensure our model would perform in any scenario it may be applied and regardless of the user.

As we continue to iterate on our machine and the website it will reside on, we will work to implement the lessons of implicit bias and hegemony that we have learned. Buolamwini defined the coded gaze as the practice of training models and algorithms to reflect the same implicit biases and prejudices that have existed for centuries. In our development, we will continue to consider how to combat our own implicit biases as three white, male developers to not recreate them in our teachable machine's dataset, and in turn the machine itself.

Throughout this project, we have had first-hand experience with the fact that bias cannot be eliminated. Initially, we assumed that being aware of and working around potential bias in our model would let us create a much less biased dataset. However, we have found that every stage of the development process involved more choices that reflected our own experiences and limitations, introducing further bias into our design. The skin tones represented in our dataset, the environment at UW where we all attend, how we defined the hand signs to use, and even the types of cameras used to create our dataset have all introduced more potential sources of bias. As Buolamwini states in Unmasking AI, the goal is not to pretend these biases do not exist but rather to identify them and take deliberate steps to minimize their impact.

To improve our model, the first step would be to make our dataset training the model larger and more diverse, ideally with participants from a broad range of racial and ethnic background, especially individuals with darker skin tones, to make the model perform more consistently across different users. Then, more extensive testing of the model with users who are unfamiliar with it would provide us with whether using the model is straight-forward for anyone, and other ways we can improve upon it.
